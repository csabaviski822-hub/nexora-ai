Beyond Vision: How T-Rex Is Teaching Robots to Feel

Human hands can sense pressure, slipping and texture. Robots have traditionally relied heavily on cameras. T-Rex explores how tactile feedback can help robots react more precisely when handling objects.

What Is T-Rex?

On November 5, 2026, Panasonic announced Tactile-Reactive Dexterous Manipulation, known as T-Rex. The research combines visual and language-based action generation with tactile information from sensors on a robot's fingertips.

Why Does Touch Matter?

Imagine picking up an egg. A robot needs to apply enough force to hold it, but not so much that it breaks. It must also react if the object starts to slip. Cameras alone cannot always provide all the information needed for this task.

T-Rex combines a vision-and-language module with a faster tactile correction process. The system can use contact feedback to adjust its movements while carrying out a task.

Promising Test Results

According to Panasonic, T-Rex was evaluated on 12 real-world tasks involving delicate force control and object handling. It achieved an average success rate of 65%, compared with 35% for the strongest baseline method reported in the announcement.

When tactile information was removed, the average success rate fell to 42%. This suggests that touch feedback can make a meaningful difference in precise robotic manipulation.

What Can These Robots Do?

The evaluated tasks included turning pages, transferring eggs, applying toothpaste, unlocking with a key, pulling out a card and screwing in a light bulb. These activities require careful contact and force control.

Where Could This Technology Be Used?

  • Manufacturing: inserting and tightening delicate components.
  • Logistics: handling fragile or flexible products.
  • Industrial assistance: performing tasks that require precise force.
  • Research: building robots that interact more effectively with objects.

The Road Ahead

T-Rex is a research development, not proof that robots can already perform every delicate human task independently. Reliability, safety, cost and adaptation to unfamiliar objects remain important challenges.

Still, combining sight, language and touch could be an important step toward robots that interact with the physical world more effectively.
